Sample size calculator for UX research

Work out how many participants you need to estimate a score precisely, to detect a difference between two designs, or to find most usability problems.

Free and private: everything runs in your browser and nothing you enter or upload is sent anywhere.

How it is calculated

  1. Estimating a score: n = (t × SD ÷ margin)², recalculated until n settles, because the t value depends on n.
  2. Comparing two designs: n = k × ((t for confidence + t for power) × SD ÷ difference)², with k = 2 for different people per group and k = 1 for the same people (within-subjects). Again iterated on t.
  3. Finding problems: the chance of seeing a problem at least once with n users is 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ, so n = ln(1 − goal) ÷ ln(1 − p).

References

  1. Sauro, J., & Lewis, J. R. (2016). Quantifying the User Experience: Practical Statistics for User Research (2nd ed.). Morgan Kaufmann.
  2. Nielsen, J., & Landauer, T. K. (1993). A mathematical model of the finding of usability problems. Proceedings of INTERCHI 1993, 206–213. doi.org/10.1145/169059.169166
  3. Cohen, J. (1988). Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences (2nd ed.). Lawrence Erlbaum.

Frequently asked questions

How many users do I need for a usability test?

It depends on the goal. To find problems that affect about a third of users, five users give roughly an 85% chance of seeing each one, which is where the famous "five users" figure comes from. Problems that affect fewer people need more users. To report a score such as SUS with a margin of ±5 points at 95% confidence you need around 50 people, and comparing two designs usually needs more.

How many participants do I need for a SUS study?

With the typical SUS standard deviation of about 17–18 points, roughly 50 participants give a 95% confidence interval of ±5 points, and about 15 give ±10 points. Use the "Estimate a score" mode above with your own margin.

What standard deviation should I use?

The best source is your own earlier data on the same measure. Without that, published values are a reasonable starting point: SUS scores usually have a standard deviation of about 17–18 points. A larger standard deviation always means you need more participants.

What is statistical power?

Power is the chance that a study detects a real difference of the size you specified. 80% is the usual convention (Cohen, 1988), so one study in five would miss a true effect of exactly that size. Raising power to 90% needs about a third more participants.
